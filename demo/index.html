Surrogate modelling of airfoil lift and drag, and what happens at stall

Riya C.

A project report. A machine-learning model was trained on 800 CFD solutions of airfoils, used inside a design search, and then tested where it should fail: through stall, against wind-tunnel data.

1The question

An airfoil is the cross-section of a wing. Its lift coefficient Cl and drag coefficient Cd depend on its shape, on the angle of attack α, and on the Reynolds number Re, which compares inertial and viscous forces in the flow. Designers find Cl and Cd by solving the Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) equations, a computer simulation (CFD) that takes about 25 minutes per case on 16 processor cores. A design search needs thousands of cases.

A surrogate is a fast statistical model fitted to a set of those solutions. This project asks two things: can a surrogate stand in for the solver inside a design search, and how far can it be trusted when the flow separates from the wing, which is what happens at stall?

At small angles, lift rises in a straight line with α. Near 15 to 18° for a symmetric section the flow separates from the upper surface, lift falls, and drag jumps. A model fitted only to attached flow has no way to know that this threshold exists. That is the central observation of the project, and sections 4 to 6 test it.

2Data

AirfRANS (Bonnet, Mazari, Cinnella and Gallinari, NeurIPS 2022). The dataset stores the flow fields, so lift and drag were computed here by integrating pressure and wall shear stress over the surface of each airfoil.

Solutions1,000 steady RANS solutions: 800 for training, 200 held out (the dataset's published split). 489 four-digit and 511 five-digit NACA sections.
SolverOpenFOAM v2112, simpleFoam, k-ω SST turbulence model, wall-resolved
Flow2D, steady, incompressible; Re 2 to 6 million; Mach below 0.3
Angle of attack−5° to 15°. The upper limit was chosen by the dataset authors to avoid stall.
Almost no stalled flow is in this dataset. That is a design choice of the authors, and it is what makes the stall question interesting.

3Method

Inputs and outputs

Each case is described by angle of attack, log Reynolds number, maximum thickness, maximum camber and its position, camber at 75% chord, the thin-airfoil zero-lift angle, and the effective angle α − αL0. The model predicts Cl and log10(Cd). Drag is predicted on a log scale because it is always positive and spans more than a decade.

Models

Three families were trained and compared: random forest, support-vector regression with an RBF kernel (SVR), and a multilayer perceptron (MLP).

Checking

  • The published 800/200 split is used unchanged. The 200 test cases are never used for tuning.
  • Settings are tuned by nested 5-fold cross-validation, with all cases of one geometry kept in the same fold so that a shape never appears in both training and validation.
  • Errors are reported overall and for α ≥ 10°. Drag error is given in drag counts (1 count = 0.0001).
  • A second task trains on −2.5° to 12.5° only and tests outside it, to measure extrapolation in angle.

5Design optimisation

A genetic algorithm searched over camber, camber position, thickness and angle of attack at Re = 4 million to maximise predicted Cl/Cd. The bounds are the sampling ranges of the dataset. Five independent runs were compared with a random-search baseline of the same budget: 5,980 surrogate evaluations per search.

Cost, with its assumptions

5,980 CFD solutions at 25 minutes each is about 104 days, against 2.7 seconds. That is an estimate for the same number of evaluations. A real CFD optimiser would use a sample-efficient method and far fewer runs, so the ratio overstates the practical advantage. The surrogate also has a one-off cost the comparison leaves out: generating the training data, about 17 days at that rate. The break-even is therefore around 1,000 evaluations.

How much to trust the optimum: not much yet. It is a prediction of the surrogate, not a CFD-verified design. The three models disagree on the best shape. The SVR and MLP optima sit on the edge of the sampled design space, where models are least constrained. The genetic algorithm beats random search by only 1 to 2% in final value; its benefit is speed of convergence. The next step is to run the proposed section through OpenFOAM.

6Testing against experiment, through stall

The models were compared with NASA wind-tunnel measurements of the NACA 0012 at Re = 6 million (Ladson, NASA TM 4074; 53 points from −4° to 19.3°) and evaluated well past the 15° limit of the training data.

In the experiment, lift peaks at Cl = 1.635 at 17.2°. At 18° and above it has fallen to about 1.0 to 1.2, and drag has jumped from about 0.025 to between 0.19 and 0.43. Stall takes place within one degree.

  • Inside the range, all three agree with experiment to within a few hundredths in Cl.
  • Beyond the range but before stall, the SVR and MLP still track the experiment, because the attached-flow trend simply continues.
  • At stall, all fail. At the six stalled points the models predict Cl of about 1.55 (forest), 1.67 (SVR) and 1.80 (MLP) against a measured mean of 1.10. Predicted drag is 0.026 to 0.038 against 0.28 measured, an under-prediction by a factor of 7 to 11.
  • The forest's own uncertainty does not warn. The spread across its trees rises by only about 30% at the stalled points (0.19 to 0.25), while its actual error rises more than twenty-fold. The spread is roughly constant and carries no information about stall.

Why

As α increases, the suction peak steepens the adverse pressure gradient, the boundary layer thickens, and wall shear falls to zero: the flow separates. That changes the structure of the flow, not just its slope. Where it happens depends on the pressure distribution, which is a result of the flow and not an input to the model. A model fitted only to attached flow has no information about the threshold, whatever its type, and continues the trend. Details are in the physics primer.

What this does and does not show. The post-stall evidence is six points from one experiment: a clear illustration, not a statistical test. Near stall, the gap between surrogate and experiment mixes the surrogate's extrapolation error, the error of RANS itself in separated flow, and the tunnel's departure from two dimensions. This comparison cannot separate them. Doing so needs stall-resolved CFD labels at the same conditions.

8Limitations

  • Two-dimensional, steady, incompressible flow; NACA 4- and 5-digit sections only. Expect poor transfer to other shapes.
  • 1,000 training solutions, whose labels come from a RANS turbulence model with its own error, largest in separated flow.
  • The training data stop at 15°, so "high angle of attack" here means 10° to 15°, not stall.
  • One experiment (NACA 0012, Re = 6 million) is used for validation.
  • The optimum has not been verified with CFD. The CFD cost is an estimate from the dataset paper, not a measurement, and the timings come from a 2-core cloud machine.
